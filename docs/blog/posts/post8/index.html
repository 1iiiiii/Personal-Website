Published

November 2, 2026

A widely held thesis about holding gold is that it protects us when inflation is high. The reason is that money loses purchasing power with high inflation, while gold is a physical asset whose price should rise with everything else. Between 2021 and 2023, that thesis got its biggest test in four decades. 12-month inflation in the Consumer Price Index (CPI) reached 8.98% in June 2022, the highest since 1981.1 Yet, from the end of 2020 to that peak, consumer prices rose 12.6% while gold fell 4.6%. Then, as inflation cooled in 2024 and 2025, gold more than doubled. If gold were an inflation hedge, the pattern should have been the reverse.

This matters now because CPI inflation is still running at 3.7% (year to September 2026) and we are experiencing an energy shock. The 2026 oil price shock produced three of the 29 largest monthly CPI increases since 2003. Anyone holding gold as insurance against another inflation wave is betting that it does better when inflation is high. If it does not, Treasury inflation-protected securities (TIPS), whose principal is indexed to the CPI, do that job by contract. The question the blog post asks is: across 2003 to 2026, does gold do better than usual when inflation is high?

Test statistic and hypotheses

We flag the 10% of months with the highest CPI prints, where a print is the month’s inflation rate. These are the 29 of the 282 months, each at 0.54% or more (equivalent to 6.7% at an annual rate). Then

D = \bar{r}^{\,\text{gold}}_{\text{flagged}} - \bar{r}^{\,\text{gold}}_{\text{other}}

where the two terms are gold’s average monthly return in the flagged months and in the other 253. Gold returned +0.08% a month in the months with highest inflation and +1.05% in the others, so D = −0.97 percentage points per month.

The null hypothesis H₀: D = 0 means a high-inflation month is no different for gold than any other month, while the alternative H₁: D ≠ 0 means it is different, in either direction. We compare with other months rather than zero because gold rose about 1% in an ordinary month: against zero, +0.08% looks like gold held its value; against its own baseline, it did a point worse than usual. We also test the 12-month inflation rate, as well as the months when expected inflation (measured by the gap between nominal and inflation-protected Treasury yields), rose or fell most.

Use bootstrap to measure how precisely D is

With only 29 flagged months, −0.97 may be measured loosely, and we couldn’t know whether it is due to chance or not. Thus, we use bootstrap to randomly select measurements from the original sample, allowing the same measurement to be selected multiple times. For each bootstrap sample, we calculate the D, and we repeat the process many times to get the spread of Ds.

Figure 1. Bootstrap distribution of D, gold’s return in the flagged months minus its return in other months.

The histogram shows the distribution of D as resample goes from 100 to 10000 times. The histogram starts being ragged, and gradually smooth out as we increase the number of resamples. More resamples remove simulation noise but add no information beyond the 29 months. At 10000 times, the 95% interval (shaded band) sits between −2.8 and +0.9 pp. It’s noteworthy that the 95% interval straddles zero, so we cannot reject the null hypothesis. This indicates that gold’s high-inflation months are not distinguishable from its ordinary ones. And if anything, it actually leans towards the negative direction, with D sitting close to -1pp.

Check the 95% bootstrap interval

We use simulation to check whether the interval from Figure 1 actually catches the true D 95% of the time. We treat our 282 months as the whole population, so the true D is −0.97. Then, we draw a fresh 24-year history from it, build a 95% bootstrap interval from that history alone, and repeat 1,000 times. Each line on the left panel is one simulated history’s interval, with a dot at that history’s own estimate. The lines are sorted by the estimate, so that the intervals that miss the truth (rust) sit at the two ends. The result on the right panel shows that using 24 years of data, 93% of the 1,000 cover the true D, which is close to 95%. Selecting other lengths from the dropdown shows how the share changes. The right panel plots the share of the simulations that cover the truth using different windows. Five years of data has the lowest share, 88%, because six flagged months usually look calmer than the full set, but from 10 years on it is 93–94%. As such, an interval built from a short history is less confident than it reports.

Figure 2. Left: 100 of the 1,000 simulated histories, each drawn from the 282 months with replacement, sorted by the history’s own estimate of D. Right: the share of all 1,000 intervals that contain the true D, by years of history, against the nominal level.

Permutation test on null distribution, rejection region and p-value

If H₀ is true, high inflation is just a label on 29 months of the sample, and it should not matter which 29. So we shuffle the labels 10,000 times, recomputing D each time. The grey histogram is what D looks like when nothing is going on. The rejection region (rust) is its outer 5%, where D’s so far from zero that chance produces them only one time in twenty (beyond about ±1.9 pp). The p-value is the share of shuffled D’s at least as extreme as the actual one. At −0.97, p = 0.32, so we do not reject H₀ at the 1%, 5% or 10% level; nor do the 12-month rate (p = 0.79) or “expectations fell” (p = 0.21). The closest scenario is when “expected inflation rose most,” and gold returned +2.36% a month against +0.82% otherwise (D = +1.55 pp, p = 0.11 two-sided, 0.05 one-sided).

Figure 3. Distribution of D when the labels are shuffled at random 10,000 times; the rust bins are the two-sided rejection region at the chosen significance level, the vertical line the actual D.

Why would gold lean negative when inflation runs hot? The highest prints in this sample come from supply shocks, such as oil in 2008, 2021–22 and the energy shock of 2026. In those months expected inflation may rise, but the Fed responds by raising rates, so nominal yields rise by more and the real yield (the nominal yield minus expected inflation) goes up. Morgan Stanley puts it as gold “struggles in supply shocks, especially where inflation expectations and bond yields rally”,2 and my previous post found gold falls about 3.5% for each percentage point the 10-year real yield rises.

Caveats

  • Months are treated as independent, but volatility clusters in time, so the intervals are probably too narrow. Using a block bootstrap would widen them, which would only make the non-rejection more secure.
  • Not rejecting H₀ is not proof that D = 0. With 29 months, the test can only detect a large difference.
  • In 19 of the 30 months when expected inflation rose most, real yields also fell, and gold’s gain sits there (+3.76% a month against −0.05% in the other 11). That result may be the real-yield channel, not demand for an inflation hedge.
  • CPI for a month is published in the middle of the next, so the high-inflation months are known only after gold’s return; the breakeven version has no such lag.

Takeaway

From 2003 to 2026, gold showed no sign of hedging the inflation that actually arrived. It returned 0.08% a month in the 29 highest-inflation months against 1.05% otherwise, a gap within what chance produces (p = 0.32). The only hint of a hedge comes when expected inflation rises (p = 0.11), and even that gain sits in months when real yields fell. A hypothesis that fits both results and my previous post is that gold behaves as a real-yield asset, so rising inflation expectations help it only when they pull real yields down. With the 10-year breakeven between 2.15% and 2.46% since mid-2024, expectations have barely moved. As Morgan Stanley notes, September’s rise in yields came almost entirely from real yields, with breakevens up just 4 basis points, and “relatively stable inflation expectations limit its appeal as an inflation hedge”.3

Data and code

  • Gold: COMEX gold futures, front-month continuous contract (GC=F), monthly return from month-end closes, built for Post 7 from Yahoo Finance daily data via yfinance (accessed 24–25 October 2026)
  • Inflation print: Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, all items, seasonally adjusted (CPIAUCSL, Bureau of Labor Statistics), monthly, via FRED; the month’s inflation is the percentage change in the index.
  • Expected inflation: 10-year breakeven inflation rate (T10YIE, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis), month-end value via FRED
  • Period: February 2003 to October 2026 (CPI to September 2026); FRED series accessed 31 October 2026.
