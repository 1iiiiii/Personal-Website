What are you holding when you hold gold?

After its peak at $5,318 per ounce in January, gold price has dipped to about $4,000 right now, marking a drawdown of about 25%. Because gold is non-yielding (i.e. holding it does not generate any interest), the opportunity cost of holding gold is the interest a saver could earn by lending to the government. Given recent increase in yield on 10-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (10Y TIPS), which is a Treasury bond whose principal adjusts to inflation and rise in energy price, the drop in gold price seems reasonable. Yet, when we zoom out a little, the price of gold has actually doubled since 2021, while the 10Y TIPS rate rose 3.6 percentage points. This seems to contradict the theory that there is an inverse relationship between gold and the real interest rate.

Other factors seem to dominate the movement, at least periodically. Usually, there are three candidates other than real interest rate: dollar, stocks, and oil. I regress gold’s weekly return on all four at once, so each number below is one factor’s effect with the other three held fixed. This post tries to answer: which of these exposures does the price of gold carry, how big is each, and does that change after 2022? Understanding the correlations and movements can be important to central banks, especially those in the emerging market, as they divert away from U.S. government bonds and dollars when Russian accounts were frozen. Knowing which risk gold actually carries is also pertinent to anyone who wishes to invest in assets with relatively stable long-term return, since gold is a precious physical good with inherent value and no credit risk.

Gold’s exposure to dollar, real yield, stock, and oil

The interactive figure below gives a rolling sensitivity analysis of gold against other factors, with a two-standard-error confidence band. Click the dropdown menu to select the factor/asset.

  • Dollar. Over the past twenty years, for every 1% increase in dollar index, gold price drops by 1.6% on average. The exposure remains negative and has barely exceeded 0 even for the upper bound of the interval. There is a very clear and persistent negative link between dollar and gold.
  • Real yield.A one-point rise in the 10-year real yield lowers gold by 3.5% with the dollar held fixed. Real yields and the dollar usually rise together, though. If we count the stronger dollar that comes with real yield, a one-point rise has gone with a 6.3% fall in gold. The link between real yield and gold is rather unstable as the two-year estimate swings from -14 to +6.
  • Stock. Before 2022, a 1% fall in the S&P 500 came with a 0.2% rise in gold, making gold a weak hedge. Since 2022, the estimate is zero. This makes sense as commodities have historically exhibited low correlation with equities.
  • Oil. The direct exposure of gold to oil is close to zero in every window. Yet, energy price shock affect gold price indirectly through real yields and dollar. Before 2026 oil did not move real yields, but in 2026 an oil jump pushed real yields and the dollar up, and gold fell about 1% for every 10% rise in Brent.
  • Drift. There is also the return none of the four factors explains, which is about 11% a year before 2022 and 21% since. Figure 3 will return to it.

Figure 1. Two-year rolling regression of gold’s weekly return on the four factors

Is gold still a safe asset?

Figure 2 plots gold’s monthly total returns against three types of portfolio. The rust dots are the the worst 10% of months for a portfolio, and the blue dots are the rest of the months. When we hover over the dots, we can see the return for both in each month. The dropdown menu allows us to select each portfolio. For the portfolio with 60% stock ETF and 40% Treasuries, the average return in the worse 10% of the month is -4.4%. In those months gold averaged -0.9% and rose 48% of the time, below its usual 55%. If we switch the dropdown to pure S&P500 ETF, before 2022 gold rose in 76% of the worst equity months, and since 2022 in only 2 of 8 (too few sample to draw any conclusion yet). Finally, for the pure bond portfolio, gold fell 2.9% on average in the worse Treasury months. The rate exposure from Figure 1 is showing up exactly when bonds lose money.

Figure 2. Monthly total returns from month-end prices, February 2003 to October 2026

Where the returns actually came from

Figure 3 decomposes the contribution of each of the factors to gold’s yearly return. The colored pieces are what the four exposures account for, and grey is the unexplained factor. We could hover over the bars to see the detailed decomposition. Clicking a legend entry can remove a component and allows us to see what remains.

In the past five years, the unexplained factor accounts for a significant proportion of gold’s return. In 2024 and 2025, the grey bar is about twice the size of any year before 2022.In 2022, real yields and dollar both rose, and they together contributed to -17 log points to gold’s return. However, gold price ended flat because the the drift added +13 points, offsetting the downward pressure from higher yields and stronger dollar.

More strikingly, in 2024, gold rose 27% against the backdrop of stronger dollar, and +44 log points was due to the unexplained factor. 2025 continued the trend, with the drift contributing 55 points.

Figure 3. Each factor’s contribution is its full-sample coefficient x the factor’s cumulative move that year, in log points so the pieces add up to the year’s return

What might the drift be?

Prices cannot say what the drift is, but there are two candidates. Central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes of gold a year in 2022-24, over twice their 2010-21 average of 473;1 a buyer who ignores TIPS yields and the dollar shows up here as pure drift. But their buying slowed to 863 tonnes in 2025, the year the drift peaked, while gold ETFs took in 801 tonnes.2 So central banks fit 2022-24, and investors fit 2025 better. Both remain hypotheses.

Takeaway

Holding gold is first a bet against the dollar (1.6% per 1%, steady for twenty years) and second a bet against real yields (6.3% per point, half of it through the dollar). Oil enters only through those two, and there is not direct link between oil and gold. Gold cannot be insurance for a 60/40 portfolio because it rose in 48% of the worst months and falls when bonds fall. And since 2024 these exposures explain none of its rise in gold. The 120% came from a drift that prices alone cannot explain, and that turned negative in 2026.

Data and code

Gold: COMEX gold futures, front-month continuous contract (GC=F), daily close in US dollars per troy ounce; stocks: SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY); bonds: iShares 7–10 Year Treasury ETF (IEF), both dividend-adjusted — all from Yahoo Finance via yfinance. Real yield: 10-year Treasury inflation-indexed constant-maturity yield (DFII10, Federal Reserve Board H.15); dollar: nominal broad trade-weighted dollar index (DTWEXBGS, Federal Reserve Board); oil: Brent crude spot (DCOILBRENTEU, EIA) — all from FRED. Daily data from 2 January 2003 (the dollar index from 2 January 2006, where the four-factor regressions start) to 22 October 2026; accessed 24–25 October 2026. Regressions use weekly (Friday-to-Friday) changes with Newey–West standard errors; the hedge test uses monthly total returns from month-end prices; the 60/40 portfolio is 60% SPY and 40% IEF, rebalanced monthly.

Code, data and every number quoted above: github.com/1iiiiii/Gold-vs.-Real-Yield.

## What might the drift be?

There are many candidate explanations for the drift, and the post is only
making a tentative approach to explain two of the candidates. First, 
central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes of gold
a year in 2022-24, over twice their 2010-21 average of 473.[^wgc24] Central bank 
is a buyer who ignores TIPS yields and the dollar, so Central Bank purchase raises 
gold’s average return without changing how gold reacts to those factors. The increase
in purchase from central banks was probably due to security reasons, as the U.S. froze 
Russia's account and imposed a series of sanctions after the outbreak of war. This is why
it was mainly central banks from emerging markets that were making the purchases.

Second, 
gold ETFs took in 801 tonnes[^wgc25] in 2025 when central banks buying
slowed to 863 tonnes. This signifies that investors flow started to support gold as well. 
As such, the main driver of the drift was central bank purchase from 2022-2024, and 
2025 was a combined force from both investors and central banks.
